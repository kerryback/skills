# Human-write

The default register is academic prose — journal articles, working papers, referee
reports, grant proposals, scholarly essays. Treat that as home. When a request is
plainly something else (a casual post, marketing copy), the principles still hold,
but write to the scholarly standard unless the user steers you elsewhere: formal,
precise, contractions rare, argument over ornament.

The goal is prose a good journal editor would pass, not prose that dodges a
detector. Those usually coincide: the things that make text read as machine-written
— every sentence the same length, a transition word bolted to the front of each one,
a paragraph that states its topic then restates it — are also just weak scholarly
writing. Fix the writing and the machine signature goes with it.

Two things to hold onto before any of the specifics below:

Variation is a byproduct, never a target. You do not sprinkle in a short sentence
every fourth line, or quota a fragment per paragraph. That only trades one formula
for another, and a reader feels the metronome either way. Real variation falls out
of letting each sentence be exactly as long as its thought — subordinate a clause
because the idea is genuinely subordinate, cut to three words because the point
lands hard. Shape follows meaning. Everything that follows is a way of noticing when
it hasn't.

And you preserve what the text is saying. Every factual claim, citation, quotation,
number, and the author's voice and level of formality survive the pass untouched.
You are changing how it reads, not what it asserts.

## Drafting vs. editing — same principles, two moments

When drafting fresh: compose with these habits from the first sentence. Don't
generate flat, uniform prose and plan to fix it later — the flatness is easiest to
avoid at the keyboard, hardest to sand off afterward.

When editing a draft (your own or the user's): read it once for the tells below,
then revise only where a tell actually fires. Don't rewrite a sentence that is
already working just to prove you were here.

Either way, the finished text has to clear the same bar. The checklist at the end
applies to both.

## Why AI prose reads uniform

Machine-written text regresses to the mean of everything it has seen. The symptoms
are specific and worth naming, because you catch them by name:

- Sentences cluster around one length — usually 15–25 words — and march in the same
  subject-verb-object order.
- Every paragraph is a self-contained unit: topic sentence, two of support, a
  closing sentence that restates the topic. Read the first and last line of each and
  you've read the piece.
- Connective tissue is overbuilt. However, Therefore, Moreover, Additionally,
  Furthermore, and Importantly do work that a semicolon, a juxtaposition, or nothing
  at all would do better.
- The balanced-contrast tic: "It's not X, it's Y." "Rather than X, Y." "Not only X
  but also Y." Once you see it you see it everywhere, because the model reaches for
  it as a default rhythm, not because the contrast is real.
- The rule of three, compulsively — every list has exactly three items, every
  description three adjectives.
- Hedging and throat-clearing that commits to nothing.

## The tells — words and phrases that give it away

Delete or replace on sight, unless the author genuinely talks this way:

- Inflated verbs and nouns: delve, underscore, leverage (as a verb), foster,
  navigate (the landscape), unlock, harness, tapestry, realm, testament to, treasure
  trove.
- Empty intensifiers and modifiers: robust, seamless, powerful, vibrant, crucial,
  pivotal, rich (history), very, extremely, incredibly, truly.
- Filler openers: "It's worth noting that," "It's important to remember," "At its
  core," "In today's world," "In recent years," "When it comes to."
- Skeleton phrases: "plays a crucial role in," "a testament to," "stands as," "serves
  as a reminder," "From X to Y," "Whether you're … or …."
- Closers that add nothing: "In conclusion," "Ultimately," and the summary paragraph
  that restates what the reader just read.

The em dash is not a tell in itself — it's a fine mark — but a fixed cadence of one
ornamental em dash per paragraph is. Vary the punctuation that carries your asides:
commas, parentheses, a colon, a full stop.

## What to do instead

Vary rhythm because the ideas vary. A dense claim earns a long sentence with room
for its qualifications. The consequence that matters earns a short one. Put them next
to each other and the short one hits.

Let paragraphs be the size of their idea. Some run a single sentence. Some run
twelve. Never trim or pad one to match the neighbors.

Prefer juxtaposition to signposting. Two sentences set side by side usually imply
"and so" or "but" without the word. Reach for the connective only when a reader
would otherwise take a wrong turn — then use it without guilt.

Trade vague for concrete. Not "the strategy performed robustly" but "the strategy
returned 8% a year after costs, and stayed positive in every decade since 1970."
Concreteness is the strongest single antidote to machine prose, because models
default to the abstract.

Cut anything that only restates. If a sentence's job is to summarize the last one,
delete it and trust the reader.

## Structure above the paragraph

Uniformity hides at the document level too, not just the sentence:

- Sections don't all have to be the same length, and not every thought needs a
  heading. Headings on everything chop an argument into interchangeable tiles.
- Reach for a bulleted list only when the items are genuinely parallel and
  discrete. When ideas connect and build, prose carries them better — bullets throw
  away the logic between points. (This section is a list because these really are
  separate checks; the argument above it is not.)
- Drop the closing section that summarizes. End on the strongest point, or on what
  follows from it, not on a recap.

## Academic register — the default

This is the home setting, and scholarly prose carries its own machine signature on
top of the general one. These are not optional refinements; they are what the
default output has to satisfy:

- Kill the throat-clearing opener. "This paper investigates the relationship
  between…" and "In recent years, growing attention has been paid to…" say nothing.
  Open on the actual question or the actual finding.
- State results as findings, not as the existence of implications. Not "our results
  have important implications for asset pricing" but the implication itself.
- Signpost sparingly. "Having established X, we now turn to Y" earns its place at a
  genuine hinge in a long argument, not at every subsection break.
- Hedge only where the evidence is actually uncertain. Reflexive hedging ("may
  potentially suggest") reads as machine caution, and it weakens claims you could
  make cleanly.

Formality stays high; contractions stay rare. Human does not mean casual.

## Before and after

General:

> Before: Artificial intelligence is transforming many industries. It is changing
> how companies operate. It is also changing how people work. These changes are
> significant, and they will continue to grow over time.
>
> After: AI is remaking one industry after another. Firms are rebuilding how they
> operate around it, and workers are learning to share their jobs with it. The shift
> is already large. It's speeding up.

The contrast tic:

> Before: It's not just about speed. It's about accuracy. However, accuracy alone
> isn't enough. Therefore, cost must also be considered.
>
> After: Speed matters, but accuracy matters more — and neither is worth much if the
> price is wrong.

Academic:

> Before: In recent years, there has been growing interest in momentum strategies.
> This paper investigates the profitability of momentum. We find that momentum is
> profitable. Our results have important implications for investors.
>
> After: Momentum — buying recent winners, shorting recent losers — has drawn hard
> scrutiny since Jegadeesh and Titman (1993). We ask a narrower question: does the
> profit survive realistic trading costs? Mostly it does, though the margin is
> thinner than the gross returns let on.

Notice what each rewrite does: it combines choppy sentences, varies their length,
strips the bolted-on transitions, names the specific instead of the general, and
deletes the sentence that only announced that something was important.

## Final check

Before you're done — drafting or editing — read it back and ask:

- Do three or more sentences in a row land at nearly the same length and shape?
- Is a transition word doing work that juxtaposition already did?
- Does any contrast follow the "not X, but Y" template without a real contrast under
  it?
- Are the paragraphs all the same size? Every section? A bullet list where the ideas
  actually connect?
- Any tell-word from the list above still sitting there?
- Does a sentence merely restate its neighbor?
- Read one paragraph aloud in your head. Does it sound like a person thinking, or a
  template being filled?

Revise where the answer is yes. Leave the rest alone.

## Never

- Change a fact, number, citation, or quotation.
- Add humor, slang, or personality the author didn't put there.
- Shift the level of formality.
- Rewrite a working sentence just to look busy, or purely to "sound less like AI."
